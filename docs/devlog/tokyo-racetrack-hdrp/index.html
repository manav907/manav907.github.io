You Don't Need Unreal Engine to Build This.

The Challenge

Tokyo Race Track was a Unity HDRP proof of concept built around a simple question: was switching to Unreal Engine really necessary to achieve the visual quality the client wanted?

The client already had a team experienced with Unity. Moving everyone to Unreal for a single project would mean spending time learning new tools, adapting existing workflows, and getting the team comfortable with a different engine.

Before making that investment, we wanted to show what Unity was already capable of.

The project was built largely from scratch in Unity HDRP, with the goal of creating a visually rich racing environment that could make a convincing case for staying with the team's existing workflow.

My responsibility was building and optimizing the neon-lit city environment, finding ways to preserve its visual quality while keeping the scene practical to work with.

Building for Visual Fidelity and Performance

A dense nighttime city comes with its own challenges. Between the geometry, neon lighting, and reflective surfaces, there was a lot to balance to get the look we wanted without making the environment unnecessarily demanding.

I used a range of techniques, including occlusion culling, LODs, reflection controls, and lighting groups, to help manage rendering costs and keep the scene easy to iterate on.

The focus wasn't just on making something that looked impressive in a recording. I wanted the environment to remain practical to navigate, adjust, and work with throughout development.

That was an important part of the demonstration: showing that Unity HDRP could deliver the visual experience the client was looking for within a workflow their team already understood.

Takeaway

The point of Tokyo Race Track wasn't to prove that Unity was better than Unreal Engine.

It was to demonstrate that switching engines isn't automatically the right decision just because a project demands high visual fidelity.

By building the environment in Unity HDRP, we could demonstrate what was possible without asking an experienced Unity team to abandon its existing workflow.

For me, the project reinforced something important: visual quality matters, but so does the process of achieving it. A convincing environment is only part of the equation. Being able to build, optimize, and iterate on it efficiently matters just as much.
